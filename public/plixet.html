Designing PLIXET's Product Family
and Website

An 8 week UX design internship at Planefold, taking a construction toy system through product positioning, packaging direction, and a website redesign.

Overview

PLIXET was one product turning into a product family, and the website had to catch up.

Planefold is a design led toy company. Their first product, Snapbits, blends construction with puzzles, built from magnetic 3D blocks inspired by the Soma puzzle. During my 8 weeks there, PLIXET was evolving from a single existing set into a broader family with a new, younger line. My work spanned understanding the market, defining how the two lines should be positioned, shaping packaging and activity content, and redesigning the website end to end in Figma.

The real challenge

The existing product and the new one were about to compete for the same kids.

The existing PLIXET set was positioned for children around 6 to 7 and up. The new line was being built for younger children. On paper, that read as a clean age based split.

A key fact my mentor gave me early on: in practice, many buyers and users of the existing set were already closer to 5 to 6 years old. The two audiences already overlapped before the younger line even launched, which meant age alone could never be the thing that told the two products apart.

The actual challenge became: how do you position and communicate a new younger line alongside an existing one, without the two clashing, competing, or making each other feel redundant.

Research

The questions I started with

  • What actually changes as children grow?
  • Do younger children only want creative builds?
  • Do 5–7 year olds enjoy puzzles too?
  • Do older children still enjoy imaginative building?
  • How do we make the older set not feel like a “kids' toy”?
  • Can guided challenges and open-ended creation coexist?

Research

Once age stopped being reliable, four things came out of the research that explained why.

I studied how construction toy brands communicate age, challenge, and long term value, comparing the construction toy market, magnetic building systems, and how other brands used imagery, language, and age cues. The starting questions were plain: what actually changes as children grow, do younger kids only want creative builds, do older kids still enjoy imaginative building, can guided challenges and open ended creation coexist at all.

Major insights

Play evolves, it doesn't switch

Creative and challenge led play can coexist. As children grow, the need for complexity and progression gets stronger, it doesn't replace imagination.

Parents need the system, not the age label

Buyers needed clarity on what actually changed between the two experiences, not just a number on the box.

Play motivation was the clearer differentiator

The real distinction was less “younger vs older” and more about why a child plays at all, expression and imagination versus problem solving and mastery.

The outcomes communicate the difficulty

Simpler, recognisable outcomes make play feel accessible, while more complex and impressive builds communicate greater challenge.

The reframe

I turned a given fact into a product split: Create and Challenge.

Instead of treating the two sets independently, I helped build a shared product language around why a child plays, not how old they are.

CREATE

Build, remix, invent. Designed for younger, open ended play, focused on imagination and self expression. The experience moves from building a given character, to remixing its parts, to eventually creating something entirely new.

CHALLENGE

Designed for older, progression led play. Encourages problem solving, persistence, and mastery through puzzles and missions, protecting the product from feeling too young or simplistic.

Website, constraint first

The existing website wasn't a normal ecommerce page, and the redesign had to respect that.

Planefold's site uses scroll driven storytelling, common in immersive Three.js sites: a 3D layer animates the blocks, an HTML layer carries text and product info, and scrolling moves both together like a sequence of full screen scenes. Because scroll was already doing double duty controlling the 3D animation, adding new content meant coordinating a new 3D state each time, not just adding a section.

The primary audience, parents, needed to quickly tell the two experiences apart, understand which suited their child, and see the product's value before buying. That created a direct tension: the business side wanted a site that was clear and easy to shop, while the design side wanted to keep the immersive, interaction led feel.

The journey of discussions, questions and iterations that shaped the strategy.

Decision one

Showing both products side by side got rejected after mentor feedback.

Homepage discovery

Side by side choice, or scroll driven reveal?

Rejected: show both products side by side and let the buyer choose which to look at first. This asked the user to choose before they understood what actually made the products different.

Chosen: reveal the products one at a time through scroll, each with its own Learn More entry point. Each product gets its own visual space and both stay inside one continuous homepage journey.

Decision two

A sliding panel let the site be easy to shop without breaking the immersive feel.

Product detail navigation

How should “Learn More” work without leaving the homepage experience?

Users shouldn't get lost, shouldn't wait too long, should find everything about both products easily, and should be able to move between the two without friction.

Chosen: Each product page opens as a panel that slides up from the bottom. The homepage stays underneath the whole time, allowing users to explore the product and return to the same point by closing the panel.

Why this worked
Preserved the user’s position on the homepage
Created a smooth transition between overview and detail
Made switching between the two products easier
Avoided unnecessary page reloads
Gave every product a shareable and direct link

Design system

Typography and color came from the product itself, not a generic system.

Montserrat carries the interface and UI text, built on fluid, responsive type foundations. A bolder display face carries the brand moments, like the Play, Create, and Challenge wordmarks. The core palette comes directly from the physical Snapbits magnetic blocks.

The component library followed a simple hierarchy: a filled primary button for high priority actions, an outlined secondary button for supporting actions, and a minimal tertiary style for navigation.

Reflection

Three things this internship actually changed

Design must balance users and business

The sliding product panel is a concrete example of one interaction satisfying a business goal and a design goal at the same time, instead of picking one.

Design improves through discussion

The audience overlap insight that reframed the whole project came from my mentor. The side by side homepage version got rejected because of her feedback. Sharing work early made both turns possible.

Research is an ongoing process

Research didn't end once the brief was understood. Continuously asking the right questions mattered more than rushing toward a polished result.
